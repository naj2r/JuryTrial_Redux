# Deep Reading Notes: "Flying Blind: Voter Information and Prosecutor Behavior"

**Authors:** Siddhartha Bandyopadhyay, Bryan C. McCannon
**Journal:** Criminology, Criminal Justice, Law & Society, 2017
**Pages:** 5 pages (comment/opinion piece)
**Citation:** Bandyopadhyay & McCannon (2017)

---

## Paper Type and Context

**Comment piece** on Ronald Wright (2017) "Prosecutor Institutions and Incentives." Authors respond to Wright's claim that prosecutors "fly blind" (lack information) and "fly solo" (don't consult other criminal justice actors).

**Authors' intervention:** Distinguish between **exogenous constraints** (prosecutors can't get information) vs. **endogenous incentives** (prosecutors don't want information).

---

## Core Argument

### Distinction: Constraint vs. Incentive

**Wright's framework:**
- Prosecutors face information gaps (exogenous constraint)
- Prosecutors operate independently (institutional feature)
- Both lead to suboptimal criminal justice outcomes

**B&M response:**
These may arise **partly from endogenous incentives** rather than pure constraints:
- Prosecutors may *choose* to operate with limited information
- Prosecutors may *choose* not to collaborate with judges, defense counsel, community

**Why?** A more transparent, data-rich system is harder to manipulate and may invite criticism of prosecutorial outputs.

### Electoral Incentives Explain "Flying Blind"

**Key insight:** Given that prosecutors are **elected** in 47 states:

1. **Voters have limited information** about prosecutor quality and policy preferences
2. **Prosecutors have limited information** about voter preferences
3. **Both are flying blind**, but this **creates opportunity for electoral signaling**

**Theory citation:** Bandyopadhyay & McCannon (2014, 2015) show that with asymmetric information about prosecutor quality:
- Prosecutors can signal via trial-taking (newsworthy)
- Voters use limited metrics (trial convictions, sentences)
- Outcome: Excessive trials even when society would prefer pleas

**Extension:** If voters had **multi-dimensional information** (conviction rates, plea rates, recidivism, case backlog), prosecutors would have less incentive to distort case selection.

**But:** Prosecutors may not lobby for such transparency because it would reveal their distortions and make incompetent prosecutors replaceable.

> "Flying blind and keeping the citizenry blind may be in the best interests of the prosecutor."

---

## Key Theoretical Points

### 1. Information Asymmetry Is Bidirectional
- Voters don't know prosecutor quality
- Prosecutors don't know voter priorities
- Both conditions necessary for distortionary equilibrium

### 2. Electoral Motivation Creates Incentive to Stay Opaque
**Proposition:** Competent prosecutors benefit more from transparency (they can showcase genuine quality), but even they may resist it because:
- It exposes ALL prosecutors to scrutiny
- It enables replacement of incompetent prosecutors
- Collective action problem: individual prosecutor can't unilaterally become transparent without signaling weakness

### 3. Two Types of Reform Differ in Effectiveness

**Exogenous constraints approach (Wright):**
- Create databases of case outcomes, system impacts
- Mandate information sharing across agencies
- Pros: Directly addresses information gaps
- Cons: Prosecutors may still lack incentive to use information if it invites criticism

**Endogenous incentives approach (B&M):**
- Change election structure (appoint vs. elect; reduce reelection pressure)
- Require multi-dimensional performance metrics (not just convictions/sentences)
- Empower other actors (judges, defense counsel) to constrain prosecutorial discretion
- Pros: Aligns prosecutor incentives with information use
- Cons: May face political resistance; electoral accountability has legitimacy appeal

### 4. External Constraints and Internal Incentives Are Complements

**Mechanism:** Externally-imposed rules can shift internal incentives:
- If legislature requires prosecutors to track and report case outcomes
- Prosecutors' internal incentives may shift toward actually using this information
- Change in constraints → change in incentives → change in behavior

1. **Bandyopadhyay & McCannon (2014):** Trial-taking increases under electoral pressure (empirical; this was the 16-page Public Choice paper)

- **Besley & Coate (2008):** Issue unbundling via citizen initiatives

## How This Extends McCannon 2014

**McCannon 2014 shows:** Prosecutors distort trials when facing elections.

**This piece asks:** Why don't prosecutors object to or try to work around this distortion?

**Answer:** Because **information opacity serves their electoral interests**.

The mechanism is subtle:
1. Voters use trial convictions as quality signal (limited metric)
2. Prosecutors respond by taking more trials to appear tough
3. But if voters had better information (recidivism, plea negotiation skill, trial outcomes), prosecutors couldn't manipulate so easily
4. Prosecutors therefore have weak incentive to lobby for transparency

---

## Relation to Our Findings (Δ ≈ 0 on Dispositions)

**This paper doesn't directly test jury verdicts**, but it provides a **information-based framing** for why electoral pressure might increase trials without affecting jury acquittal rates:

**Our narrative interpretation:**
1. **Electoral pressure → prosecutors increase trials** (McCannon 2014) ✓
2. **But voters are "flying blind"** — they observe trial volume and conviction counts, not jury acquittal rates
3. **Juries are NOT responsive to electoral signals** because:
   - Jury deliberations are secret (voters don't observe jury reasoning)
   - Jury composition changes case-to-case
   - Jurors are not directly elected (most judges aren't, but prosecutors are)
4. **Result:** Prosecutors respond to electoral incentives by changing case selection, but jury behavior remains constant

**Implication for our paper:** If we find Δ ≈ 0 on verdicts, we might frame it as: "Electoral pressure affects prosecutorial selection (which cases to try) but not jury decision-making (how to decide tried cases), consistent with voters being informationally limited to publicly observable signals like trial volume."

## Theoretical Contribution

**This piece shifts focus from mechanism to incentive alignment:**

- Theory (2013): *How* does signaling distort trials?
- Empirics (2014): *Do* prosecutors actually distort trials?
- Meta-theory (2017): *Why* don't prosecutors resist this distortion via transparency?

The answer: **Transparency threatens their electoral interests**.

---

## Strengths

1. **Identifies a mechanism-design problem:** Elections create incentives for prosecutors to prefer opacity
2. **Connects to institutional design literature:** Electoral vs. appointment structures
3. **Nuances reform debate:** Not all solutions are equally effective; need to align incentives, not just provide information
4. **Accessible writing:** Clearly explains the logic without heavy math

## Weaknesses

1. **No empirical test:** Comment piece only; cites prior work
2. **Speculative incentives:** Doesn't observe prosecutor resistance to transparency; infers it from theory
3. **Limited scope:** Focuses on prosecutor elections; ignores other sources of prosecutorial discretion distortion (e.g., conviction metrics in non-elected offices)
4. **Reform discussion brief:** Doesn't deeply explore alternative election structures (term limits, appointment commissions, etc.)

---

## What It Adds to Our Understanding

1. **Establishes information problem:** Voters observe trial volume but not jury acquittal rates or case quality
2. **Incentive alignment framework:** Prosecutors may not want better information available, even if they could access it
3. **Complementarity insight:** External rules + internal incentive shifts work better than either alone
4. **Negative result on reform:** Merely creating databases won't change behavior if prosecutors have no incentive to use them

**For our paper:**
- If we observe Δ ≈ 0 on verdicts, we can cite this paper to argue that voters' limited information may explain why prosecutors still increase trials
- The "jury verdicts are invisible to voters" framing becomes important for explaining why electoral pressure affects selection but not jury behavior
