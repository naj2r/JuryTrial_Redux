# Research Notes: Hessick (2023) Pilot Study on Media Coverage of Prosecutors
**Document:** "Media Coverage of Prosecutors and Their Elections: Results of a Pilot Study"
**Published:** February 2023
**Source:** Prosecutors and Politics Project, UNC School of Law
**Director:** Prof. Carissa Byrne Hessick

---

## Executive Summary

This is a pilot study examining media coverage of prosecutor elections in 2020 across four states (Arizona, Kansas, New York, Oregon). It measures QUANTITY, CONTENT, and TONE of media coverage for 27 jurisdictions (47 candidates) using nearly 2,000 articles from print and limited TV coverage. The study finds that:

1. Media coverage is extremely sparse and varies dramatically by jurisdiction
2. Coverage quality is low—most lacks information voters need to make informed choices
3. Coverage is overwhelming neutral in tone (77%), despite rising crime
4. Incumbents receive substantially more coverage than challengers
5. Media attention is highly correlated with contestation, incumbent status, and population

**No Michigan-specific data included.** This is a four-state pilot (AZ, KS, NY, OR).

---

## Study Scope & Methodology

### Sample Design
- **States:** Arizona (6 elections), Kansas (6 elections), New York (5 elections), Oregon (10 elections) = 27 total jurisdictions
- **Candidates:** 47 total candidates (42 running in 2020, 5 non-running incumbents included)
- **Articles:** 1,974 unique articles + 2,115 total articles examined (calendar year 2020)
- **Selection criteria:** Jurisdictions chosen to achieve:
  - Mix of population ranges (< 30k to > 1 million)
  - Mix of contested vs. uncontested elections
  - Available local news sources (searchable in America's News and Lexis Nexis databases)
- **Article types coded:** 84% straight news, 7% letters to editor, 6% opinion/editorial, 2% advertisements/notifications

### Methodology
- **Coding:** Two codebooks (incumbent prosecutors, candidate prosecutors) with standardized content analysis
- **Intercoder reliability:** 82.4% agreement on second practice round (313/380 points matched)
- **Coding variables:** Mention depth, crime type, crime rates, tone, election discussion, platform/policy information
- **Data publication:** Available at UNC Dataverse (DOI:10.15139/S3/3SWIKQ)
- **Code:** Available on GitHub (https://github.com/rtburg/prosecutors_and_politics_media_pilot)

## KEY FINDINGS

### 1. QUANTITY OF COVERAGE

#### Overall Coverage Levels (Shockingly Low)
- **Incumbent median:** 24 articles across entire election year
- **Range for incumbents:** 0 to 302 articles
- **Non-incumbent median:** 19 articles
- **Key insight:** Most candidates receive minimal media attention

#### Contested vs. Uncontested Elections
- **Incumbent in contested race:** 135.5 median mentions
- **Incumbent in uncontested race:** 15 median mentions
- **9x difference** between contested and uncontested coverage

#### Incumbents vs. Challengers
- Incumbents receive more coverage than challengers in ALL contested elections
- In contested races: incumbents median 135.5 mentions vs. challengers median 17 mentions
- When challengers face non-incumbents (open seats): median rises to 27 mentions
- Presence of incumbent depresses challenger coverage

#### Population Correlation
Coverage increases with population size across all categories:
- < 30k population: very low coverage
- 30k-100k: slight increase
- 100k-250k: more visible
- 250k-1M: substantial coverage
- > 1M: highest coverage (median in New York: 31 mentions)

### 2. CONTENT QUALITY

#### Election Information (Critical Deficit)
- **77% of articles about incumbents** did NOT mention the election, even in election year
- **10% mentioned election only in passing**
- **8% provided some detail about the election**
- **Only 5% discussed candidate platforms or policies**
- Example: A.B. Felton (Polk County, OR) mentioned in 8 articles, NONE mentioned he was up for reelection

#### Type of Mention (Depth)
- **45% of incumbent mentions:** only in passing (single mention)
- **33% of incumbent mentions:** limited discussion (2-3 mentions)
- **22% of incumbent mentions:** significant discussion (3+ mentions or substantive office/policy discussion)

Non-incumbents slightly better:
- **42% in passing**
- **20% limited discussion**
- **37% significant discussion**

#### Coverage Context
- **51% of coverage** occurred in context of specific crime/case
- **5%** focused on category of crimes
- **11%** primarily about the election
- **11%** about criminal justice system generally
- **4%** primarily about prosecutor themselves

#### Crime Focus (Sensationalism)
- **40%+ of articles** mentioned violent crimes (homicide, rape/assault, shooting)
- **~25% of all articles** mentioned homicide specifically
- **9%** mentioned rape/sexual assault
- **9%** mentioned shootings/violent crimes
- **Only 15%** mentioned drug crimes, theft, or other property crimes
- **Stark imbalance:** Violent crimes covered far more than their actual frequency

#### Crime Rate Discussion (Rare)
- **Only 6%** of articles mentioned crime rates at all (100 of 1,600+)
- **58%** of those expressed concern about increasing rates
- **13%** reassured readers about decreasing rates
- Suggests media may fuel perception of rising crime

#### Prosecutorial Decisions & Policies
**Incumbent prosecutors:**
- **62%** of articles discussed NO prosecutorial decisions or office policies
- **26%** discussed particular decision (case-specific)
- **10%** mentioned general/office-wide policy
- **2%** discussed both

**Non-incumbent candidates:**
- **45%** mentioned neither background nor platform
- **23%** background only
- **11%** platform only
- **22%** both background and platform

**Two candidates** (Bruce Bendish, Matthew Ellis) received NO coverage of their policy positions despite significant article counts.

#### State Variation in Content Quality

**Arizona** (most informative):
- 31% of coverage included incumbent policy information (vs. 1% Kansas, 9% NY, 0% Oregon)
- 33% of articles had 3+ mentions of prosecutor (vs. other states lower)
- 63% failed to mention election (vs. 72-80% in other states)
- 16% positive coverage (vs. other states' lower rates)

**Kansas** (least informative):
- 55% of median incumbent coverage was just passing reference (highest)
- Only 1% of coverage mentioned incumbent policies
- Median only 11 articles per candidate
- 75% of non-incumbent coverage failed to mention background or platform

**New York** (highest volume):
- Median 31 mentions (highest in study)
- 52% of coverage mentioned prosecutor 3+ times
- Higher depth overall

**Oregon** (minimal depth):
- Only 14% of median coverage had 3+ mentions
- 89% contained no information about decisions or policies
- 100% of median incumbent coverage neutral in tone (never positive)

### 3. TONE OF COVERAGE

#### Overall Tone Distribution
- **77% neutral**
- **13% positive**
- **6% negative**
- **4% mixed positive/negative**

Non-incumbents slightly more varied:
- **74% neutral**
- **16% positive**
- **7% negative**
- **3% mixed**

#### Negative Coverage Patterns
**Correlation with population:**
- < 100k population: < 2% negative coverage
- > 1 million population: > 9% negative coverage
- 5x difference based on jurisdiction size

**Correlation with contestation:**
- Half of candidates receiving high negative coverage (> 5%) faced contested elections
- Only 1 uncontested candidate (Leavenworth, KS) had that level of negative coverage
- **Causal direction unclear:** Does contestation drive negative coverage or does negative coverage prompt challengers?

#### State-Level Tone Variation
- **Arizona:** 16% positive median (least neutral at 78%)
- **Kansas:** 6% positive, mostly neutral
- **New York:** Median incumbent 6% positive, median non-incumbent 16% positive
- **Oregon:** Median incumbent 0% positive, 100% neutral

## ELECTORAL OUTCOMES & MEDIA COVERAGE

### Contested Elections: Media Mentions & Winning

**Key finding:** "Candidates with the most media mentions won in 9 of 11 contested elections"

- **Incumbent winners** had higher coverage than their challengers in all 6 contested races with incumbents
- **Open-seat winner** always had most media mentions
- Two incumbent losers: Anthony Scarpino Jr. (Westchester, NY) and Eric Nisley (Wasco, OR)
  - Both defeated in primary despite being incumbents
  - Both received substantial coverage (203 and 18 mentions respectively)
  - Media coverage alone did not guarantee incumbency advantage in contentious races

## STATE-BY-STATE SUMMARY

### Arizona (Most In-Depth Coverage)
**6 districts studied:** Apache, Greenlee, Maricopa, Pima, Pinal, Santa Cruz

**Notable findings:**
- More substantive coverage than other states
- Higher policy discussion (31% vs. others' 0-9%)
- More election mentions (63% vs. others' 72+%)
- Median 26.5 articles per candidate
- 16% positive coverage (highest in study)

**Top coverage:** Allister Adel (incumbent, Maricopa) = 125 articles (won)

### Kansas (Sparse, Cursory)
**6 districts studied:** Butler, Leavenworth, Riley, Saline, Sedgwick, Shawnee

**Notable findings:**
- LOWEST median coverage: only 11 articles
- Most cursory coverage: 55% of median incumbent's coverage was just passing mentions
- 1% policy information (lowest)
- Very limited non-incumbent information (25% received platform/background info)
- Very low negative coverage (0-1% median)

**Top coverage:** Mike Kagay (incumbent, Shawnee) = 146 articles (won); Marc Bennett (incumbent, Sedgwick) = 106 articles (won)

**Zero coverage:** Darrin C. Devinney (incumbent, Butler) = 0 articles (won)

### New York (Most Coverage)
**5 districts studied:** Albany, Erie, Livingston, Niagara, Westchester

**Notable findings:**
- HIGHEST median coverage: 31 articles (influenced by larger county populations)
- In-depth coverage: 52% of coverage had 3+ mentions
- More discussion of prosecutor work
- More positive coverage for non-incumbents (16% positive vs. 6% for incumbents)
- State medians: 44% in-passing, 26% some detail, 22% significant discussion

**Top coverage:** John J. Flynn (incumbent, Erie) = 302 articles (won), P. David Soares (incumbent, Albany) = 259 articles (won)

**Incumbent losses:** Anthony Scarpino Jr. (Westchester) lost despite 203 articles; Toporowski (challenger) received 124 articles

### Oregon (Least In-Depth)
**10 districts studied:** Clackamas, Coos, Douglas, Hood River, Jefferson, Lake, Lane, Polk, Umatilla, Wasco

**Notable findings:**
- LOW depth: only 14% of median incumbent coverage had 3+ mentions
- NO policy information: 89% of median incumbent coverage had no decisions/policies discussed
- LIMITED election mentions: 20% of median incumbent coverage mentioned election
- 100% NEUTRAL coverage for median incumbent (never positive)
- Non-incumbents received highest positive coverage in study (20% median positive)
- Median only 14 articles

**Top coverage:** Patricia W. Perlow (incumbent, Lane) = 56 articles (won); James Cleavenger (challenger, Lane) = 12 articles (lost)

## IMPLICATIONS & MECHANISMS

### Why Elections Often Go Uncontested

The study does NOT directly explain WHY so many prosecutor elections are uncontested, but the media coverage findings suggest a mechanism:

1. **Information asymmetry:** Voters lack information about uncontested incumbents (77% of articles don't mention election, 62% discuss no decisions/policies)
2. **Low visibility:** Uncontested incumbents receive only 15 median mentions vs. 135.5 for contested incumbents
3. **Lack of platform discussion:** Even when mentioned, very few articles discuss prosecutor policies or platforms
4. **Challenger disadvantage:** Challengers systematically receive far less coverage than incumbents
5. **Voter knowledge problem:** Voters are largely unaware prosecutors are elected (per ACLU poll cited)

**Result:** Even when elections occur, voters lack information to make informed choices, and potential challengers lack media platform to contest races.

### Electoral Accountability Questions

The study raises but does not fully answer:
- If most voter information comes from media, and media coverage is sparse and lacks policy information, can elections effectively check prosecutorial power?
- Why do some uncontested elections have zero media coverage?
- Are certain types of jurisdiction (rural, small-population) systematically under-covered?
- How does 2020 compare to other election years?

## DATA LIMITATIONS & CAVEATS

1. **Geographic scope:** Only 4 states (AZ, KS, NY, OR); not nationally representative
2. **Selection bias:** Included only jurisdictions with available news sources; likely excludes smallest/most isolated districts
3. **Time period:** Calendar year 2020 (includes primary election period); limited to one election cycle
4. **Data source bias:** Relies on searchable databases (America's News, Lexis Nexis) and optical character recognition for Eugene Weekly; may miss local/very small outlets
7. **Duplicate handling:** Complex issue with wire service republications and online/print versions; approach may under/over-count depending on perspective
8. **Television:** Only limited sample of local/national TV; primarily print-focused
9. **Causal claims:** Study cannot determine causality (e.g., does negative coverage prompt challenges, or do challenges draw negative coverage?)

---

## REPLICATION STUDY FINDING (Footnote 3)

Study authors confirmed low coverage findings using 2022 data on Indiana, North Carolina, Tennessee, Texas:
- **Only 81.5% of candidates** (238/292) received one or more media mention in first six months of 2022
- **~20% of candidates** received ZERO media mentions during primary season
- Confirms that minimal coverage is not unique to 2020 or the four-state pilot

## RELEVANCE TO JENSEN & AMMONS MICHIGAN PROJECT

**Overlaps:**
- Both examine prosecutor elections and their political/incentive effects
- Both focus on how electoral pressures shape prosecutorial behavior
- Both use contestation as key variable (contested vs. uncontested)
- Both ask: what information do voters have about their prosecutors?

**Differences:**
- Hessick (2023) = media content analysis (what voters see)
- Jensen & Ammons = causal effects on jury trial outcomes (what prosecutors do)
- Hessick does NOT include Michigan data
- Hessick does NOT measure prosecutorial outputs (plea rates, jury verdicts, etc.)

**Why it matters for Jensen & Ammons:**
- Supports claim that electoral pressure creates incentives
- Provides context that most voters lack information to judge prosecutors
- Suggests media coverage may NOT be the mechanism through which electoral pressure operates (if coverage is sparse/neutral, how do voters know about prosecutorial performance?)
- Raises question: if media tells voters little about prosecutor performance, what DOES create electoral pressure? (Anecdotal cases? Community networks? Organized opposition?)

## NOTES ON MISSING ELEMENTS

This pilot study does NOT address:
- Why elections become contested (focuses only on coverage, not causation of contestation)
- Michigan prosecutor elections specifically
- Any mechanism analysis of HOW electoral pressure translates to prosecutorial behavior
- Outcomes data (verdict rates, plea rates, charging decisions)
- Long-term trends (only 2020 snapshot, though 2022 replication mentioned)
- TV coverage in depth (only "limited" sample)
