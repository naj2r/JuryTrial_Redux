## RESEARCH QUESTIONS & FINDINGS

### 1. Does the electoral pressure effect on prosecution dissipate over time?

**SHORT ANSWER: YES — strongly. Election effects declined markedly during the 1986–2006 period and DISAPPEARED entirely post-2006.**

#### Key Evidence:

**Within the main study period (1986–2006):**
- Figure 13 (pp. 40–43) shows **clear temporal decline** in election year effects on both admissions and months sentenced
  - Effects strongest in the 1980s–early 1990s (peak of "tough-on-crime" era)
  - Effects noticeably weaker by 2000s
  - This decline happens **within** the era of rising incarceration

**Mechanism for within-period decline:** Public opinion shifted
- GSS data shows: In 1980s–1994, majority of respondents said courts were "not harsh enough"
- After 1994: this fraction **declined significantly**
- By early 2000s: growing share believed courts were too harsh or about right
- **Figure 13 demonstrates**: States with greatest decline in "courts not harsh enough" sentiment also had greatest decline in election year effects

**After 2006 (post-era of rising incarceration):**
- Figure 16 (pp. 45–46) shows election effects **essentially disappeared**
- No observable increase in months sentenced as elections approach
- Slight admissions rate increase appears **spurious** (extends months after election)
- Okafor notes this post-2006 collapse assumes consistent term lengths (untested assumption)

#### Critical Nuance:
Okafor distinguishes between:
1. **Short-term cyclical effects** (election year → increased severity, then decline post-election)
2. **Baseline/long-term effects** (whether DAs maintain higher baseline severity throughout entire cycle)

The paper documents **cyclical dissipation clearly**. The baseline effects may persist — Okafor explicitly notes (p. 49) the study captures only short-term cyclical impact, not longer-term political considerations that might affect baseline behavior across entire cycle.

### 4. Does Okafor test any mechanism (signaling vs. competence)?

**SHORT ANSWER: YES — extensively, but "competence maintenance" is not the primary framing. The mechanisms tested are:**

1. **Public Sentiment / Voter Preference Responsiveness** (PRIMARY MECHANISM)
2. **Racial Bias** (county-level, distinct from ideology mechanism)
3. **Contestation/Competitiveness** (elections that are contested → larger effects)
4. **Political Ideology** (Republican counties → larger effects)
5. **Crime Salience** (media coverage of crime → public opinion → effects)

#### NO: "Competence Maintenance" Not Tested
- Okafor does NOT test the "proving competence" / "avoiding reversal" mechanism
- No analysis of:
  - Appellate reversal rates by election cycle
  - Claims history
  - Signaling through high-profile cases

#### YES: Mechanism Tests Present

**5A. Public Sentiment as the Primary Mechanism:**

*Evidence:*
- Election effects are **higher in Republican counties** (Figure 10)
  - Republican recent vote: +0.054 log points admissions (p < 0.05), +0.058 months (p < 0.05)
  - But **DA ideology itself weakly associated** with effects (DA Republican: +0.010 to +0.025, mostly not significant)
  - **Interpretation:** County voters' preferences drive behavior more than DA's own ideology

- **Contested elections show larger effects** (Figure 10, bottom)
  - Contested vs. uncontested: +0.040 log points on months sentenced (p < 0.10, not sig for admissions)
  - Suggests competitive pressure amplifies public-opinion responsiveness

- **General Social Survey (GSS) data validates mechanism** (Figure 12–13):
  - States with greatest softening of "courts not harsh enough" sentiment → greatest *decline* in election effects
  - Relationship weakly positive for admissions, more positive for months sentenced
  - **Figure 14:** Crime salience in local newspapers peaks ~1994, then declines sharply
  - **Figure 13 bottom:** Public opinion shift mirror-matches newspaper crime coverage trend (both peak 1994, both decline after)
  - Okafor interprets this as: **declining crime salience → shifting public opinion → reducing political incentives for harshness**

**5B. Racial Bias Mechanism:**

*Evidence (Table 2, pp. 36–37):*
- County-level **implicit racial bias (IAT score)** associated with punitiveness
- **Main effect on Black prisoners:** Implicit bias coefficient = +2.021 (p < 0.001)
  - Higher pro-White bias → higher baseline admissions for Black prisoners
- **Election year interaction:** Election Effect × Implicit Bias = −0.143 (p < 0.10, for admissions)
  - Suggests **ceiling effect**: When baseline anti-Black sentiment is high, less room to increase during elections
  - For White prisoners: effects are smaller overall and less responsive to bias

**Okafor's interpretation:** Two possible causal pathways (not resolved):
1. DAs themselves harbor racial bias → amplified in election years via political incentives
2. DAs respond to voter preferences (which include racial sentiment) → effect is voter-driven, not DA-driven

**Racial bias does NOT affect DAs uniformly:** Effects on Black prisoners increase with baseline bias (ceiling-limited escalation), while effects on White prisoners remain stable. This suggests racial sentiment moderates the electoral pressure mechanism.

## STUDY DESIGN & IDENTIFICATION

### Empirical Strategy:
- **Dynamic difference-in-differences** with county-level variation in election timing
- Specification (Equation 1):
  ```
  log(Y_cst) = γ_s + λ_t + Σ β_k · 1{R_ct = k} + Γ_cst + ε_cst
  ```
  where R_ct = relative time to election, β_k = coefficients relative to election year (normalized to 0)

- **Clustering:** County-level (83 clusters) standard errors, corrected for correlation across DAs and over time
- **Sample:** ~42,500 county-years, ~39 states with 4-year election cycles

### Robustness Checks (Section 5.4):
1. **Alternative TWFE weighting** (Sun & Abraham 2021) — results robust
2. **Common trends assumption:**
   - Tests for confounding from judicial, mayoral, sheriff elections (Figure 6) — effects persist when elections non-synchronous
   - Tests for arrest/crime rate confounding (Figure 7) — election effects NOT explained by changes in arrest/crime (in fact, opposite sign)
3. **Log transformations** of outcomes — shape preserved
4. **Different fixed effects** (county vs. state FE) — robust

## MAIN RESULTS SUMMARY

### Magnitude of Effects:
- **Election year on admissions:** β ≈ **0.024 log points** (on average)
  - Using signal variance (χ²): equivalent to moving **0.85 std devs** along DA behavior distribution
  - Interpretation: **50th to 80th percentile** in sentencing intensity

- **Election year on months sentenced:** β ≈ **0.031 log points**
  - Signal variance: **0.62 std devs** along distribution
  - Interpretation: **50th to 73rd percentile**

### Monthly Analysis (Figure 4–5):
- Clear upward trend in months sentenced **15 months before election through middle of election year**
- All estimates during election year > 0; many estimates post-election < 0
- Consistent with cyclical DA behavior peaking in election year, declining post-election

### By Offense Type:
- Effects **strongest for property and violent offenses**
- **Weakest for drug offenses** (less political salience?)
- Possibly due to mandatory minimum sentencing constraints on drugs

## MECHANISMS NOT TESTED (GAPS FOR YOUR RESEARCH)

Okafor's paper does **NOT** test:
1. **Jury demand / trial rates** — focuses on sentencing (plea + trial combined)
2. **Judicial responsiveness to electoral pressure** — focuses on DAs only
3. **Signaling through convictions** (reversals, overturned convictions) — focuses on outcomes only
4. **Competence maintenance** — no analysis of whether harsher sentences improve DA reelection odds
5. **Guilty plea rates vs. trial rates separately** — analyzes sentencing as outcome, not charging/plea decisions separately
6. **Verdict composition** (acquittals vs. convictions by charge type) — analyzes admissions, not verdicts

## CRITICAL LIMITATIONS & ASSUMPTIONS

1. **Post-2006 analysis uses projected elections** — assumes consistent term lengths (untested)
2. **IAT racial bias data is cross-sectional** (2000s) but applied to 1986–2006 period — assumes county bias didn't change materially
3. **Public opinion measured nationally (GSS)** but effects analyzed at state/county level — risk of ecological fallacy
4. **Confounding effects:** Mayoral & sheriff elections controlled for empirically, but may still be incomplete
5. **Plea bargaining assumed to dominate** — but Okafor notes judges rarely reject plea deals; judicial discretion effect assumed minimal
