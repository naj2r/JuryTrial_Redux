Prosecutor Elections, Mistakes,
and Appeals
Bryan C. McCannon*
Prosecutors under common-law tradition exercise a significant amount of discretion in the
criminal justice system. In the United States, the dominant form of accountability is that
prosecutors must be reelected by the voters. Recent empirical work illustrates that election
concerns open up the potential for distortion in the decision making of prosecutors.
Specifically, it has been shown that prosecutors take more cases to trial and plea bargain less
when running for reelection. Does this hawkish behavior of prosecutors lead to inaccuracies
in the criminaljustice system? A panel data set of appellate decisions in western New York is
analyzed. It is shown that if the initial felony conviction takes place in the six months prior
to a reelection and is appealed, the probability that the appellate court upholds the lower
court's decision decreases by 5.1-7.1 percentage points. Additional investigation into
the types of mistakes made is clone. Thus, the popular election of prosecutors results in
inaccurate sentences, wrongful convictions, and, consequently, successful appeals.
I. INTRODUCTION
The criminal justice system serves a valuable role in society. The decisions made affect the
accused (both the innocent and guilty), the victims, the families, and spill over to the rest
of us through the impacts on deterrence and public financing. The incentives and behav-
iors of those providing the public services demand attention to guarantee that the most
effective institutions are in place.
There has been much research done on the incentives facing judges and their
resulting behaviors.' What has been rather unstudied is the behavior of prosecutors. In the
United States, it is the prosecutors who exercise a significant amount of discretion. They
decide whether to file charges, which charges to file, whether to dismiss the case, and
whether to try the case before a jury or engage in plea bargaining (to name a few).

Prosecutor Elections, Mistakes, and Appeals
Prosecutors handle a significant number of cases. State-level prosecutors obtain 2.3 million
felony convictions each year (Perry 2006), which represents 95 percent of all such decisions
(Simmons 2004).
The dominant form of oversight for prosecutorial decision making in the United
States is popular election. Most states2 require that periodically (typically, four years) the
head prosecutor in each district runs for reelection. It is necessary to evaluate formally the
incentive effects of this retention mechanism.
Recent research has begun this task. Bandyopadhyay and McCannon (forthcoming)
investigate, theoretically, the asymmetric information problem between the incumbent
prosecutor and the voters. With limited knowledge of all the facts in each case and
without information on the skills of the prosecutor, voters are left with only the observable
results of the case. The authors show that when voters use case outcomes to make their
retention decision, the incumbent prosecutor has the incentive to distort his/her choices
to stay in office. In fact, the direction of the distortion depends on whether sentences
obtained or conviction rates are used as the voters' metric. Bandyopadhyay and
McCannon (2013) investigate, empirically, the existence of these distortions in a panel
data set from North Carolina. They find that in the year an incumbent prosecutor is
running for reelection the number of felony cases taken to trial increases, while the
number of cases plea bargained decreases. This effect is greater if there is a challenger to
the reelection bid. Furthermore, in districts where there has not been a contested election
in the past 16 years the results are reversed: fewer cases go to trial and more are plea
bargained. While Rasmusen et al. (2009) establish that state-level prosecutors respond
positively to an expansion in their resources, these papers show that they distort their
decision making to be reelected.
Given this hawkish behavior of prosecutors, one turns to the question of the quality
of the decisions being made. Through their increased efforts to obtain felony convictions
atjury trials, do more wrongful convictions arise? Are prosecutors able to obtain excessively
harsh punishments? How does the zeal to prosecute affect negotiations at the bargaining
table? Other than describing the state of the U.S. criminal justice system, the response to
these questions is important to public policy. If prosecutor elections lead to more trials,
which results in increased public expenditures and consumes time and effort of many
people, but these decisions can be shown to be accurate, the increased effort may be
leading to more deterrence and the implementation of socially optimal sanctions. Alterna-
tively, if mistakes are being made, then the welfare loss caused can be added to the resource
costs of prosecutorial elections and the optimality of the election mechanism can be called
into question.
To address this issue I analyze data from appellate court decisions. The hypothesis is
that if mistakes are indeed being made, then months later when the convicted individual
files an appeal, many of the mistakes will be identified and corrected. I collect a data set of
all appeals for criminal convictions in western New York over the years 2009, 2010, and

A small but growing literature investigates the incentives faced by prosecutors bal-
ancing work responsibilities with personal interest. It has mostly focused on U.S. Attorneys.
Boylan (2004) investigates the effect of salary on turnover and productivity. He finds that
lower salaries correlate with more turnover and reduced prosecutorial output. In a similar
study, Boylan and Long (2005) identify the effect of salary on Assistant U.S. Attorneys. If
one is practicing in an area with higher private-market salaries then s/he is more likely to
take a case to trial as trial provides experience valued by private law firms. Assistant U.S.
Attorneys in high-salary areas, consequently, are more productive, more likely to take a
position in a large law firm, and the positions experience more turnover. Along with salary
and career motivations, the objectives of prosecutors in office have been explored. Glaesar
et al. (2000) analyze the preferences of prosecutors by studying the federalization of drug
law enforcement. Boylan (2005) provides evidence that federal prosecutors gain from
longer prison sentences, rather than conviction rates. These valuable studies, though, focus
on the personal gain and motivations of federal prosecutors who do not face election
pressures.
With regard to state-level prosecutors, a few studies have investigated their incentives.
Gordon and Huber (2002) address, theoretically, how elections can provide strong incen-
tives to exert the proper amount of effort. Rasmusen et al. (2009) identify how resources
affect decision making; again in a framework where effort considerations are primary. Some
evidence arises, though, that prosecutors in states with popular elections behave differently
than those who are appointed. Wright (2009) comments on the lack of success of electoral
accountability in state-level prosecutor elections by analyzing data on media coverage of
these races. A DEA framework is employed by Gorman and Ruggiero (2009) to measure the
productivity of prosecutors. They, though, do not account for electoral incentives. Finally,
Pfaff (2012) highlights patterns in the criminal justice system in the United States. He
argues that the explosion in the prison population is due to an increase in the proportion
of arrests in which prosecutors choose to file. Filings are not considered here, but this work
is not exploring the incentives of the prosecutor.
One noteworthy study addresses the election influences on judges and prosecutors.
Sobel et al. (2010) consider a data set of 109 known wrongful convictions. They illustrate

II. DATA
The hypothesis to be tested is that appeals of criminal convictions that occur during the
same time period as an incumbent prosecutor's reelection campaign are more vulnerable
to mistakes and experience a decreased rate of being upheld. To test this theory, data are
collected from appellate decisions in western New York.
In the state of New York, felonies are prosecuted in either the County Court or in the
Supreme Court, which is the state's (rather than county's) court of first instance.' County
Court judges are selected in partisan, county-wide elections and serve 10-year terms.
Supreme Court justices are selected in partisan, district-wide elections and serve 14-year
terms. In more populated areas such as Erie County, which includes the city of Buffalo, the
judicial district is the county. In less-populated areas, these judicial districts include multi-
ple counties. Every area of the state is covered by one County Court and one Supreme
Court,4 but may have more than one justice operating at each.
The appeal of a felony conviction that arises at either of these two courts is taken to
the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court. The state is partitioned, geographically, into
four departments. These four departments make up the Appellate Division. The First
Department of the Appellate Division of the Supreme Court rules on appeals of decisions
originating in Manhattan and the Bronx. The Second Department covers the rest of New
York City and the greater metropolitan area. The Third Department covers central and
northern New York State. The Fourth Department, which is the subject of investigation
here, covers the counties in western New York.
A number of appellate court justices serve in each department. They are appointed
by the governor from the pool of Supreme Courtjustices to serve four-year terms. For each
case, typically five appellate justices rule on an appeal, but occasionally the number is
reduced to four. When the panel makes a ruling, a slip opinion is published, which includes
a brief response to the appellant's arguments and contains a decision.

The slip opinions of the Fourth Department of western New York are collected for
the years 2009, 2010, and 2011.5 A total of 1,874 observations, then, make up the data set.'
The observations are spread over 22 counties. From each slip opinion, a number of
observable variables can be created. There is a rich literature conducting content analysis
of judicial opinions. See Hall and Wright (2008) for an overview describing debates and
techniques, Evans et al. (2007) for a discussion of methods, and Edwards and Livermore
(2009) for potential pitfalls.
First, the primary dependent variable of interest is whether the appellate court
upholds the lower court's decision. Let UPHELD equal 1 if no change to the lower court's
decision is made. Table A3 in the Appendix provides information on the adjustments made
by the appellate justices. Typically, the convicted defendant makes the appeal. Rarely,
though, the prosecutor's office files an appeal. These appeals are typically based on pretrial
dismissals.' Let APPELLANT equal 1 if the appellant is the defendant.
To measure the complexity of the case, a straightforward proxy is employed. Let PAGES
be the number of pages of a slip opinion. Presumably, a more complicated case requires
more justification, citations, footnotes, and the like by the appellate justices. Since the
formatting of the slip opinions is consistent, the number of pages is expected to correlate with
the number of issues to be addressed, the complexity of the issues, and whether concurrence
opinions are added. More complicated cases are expected to experience a lower rate of being
upheld, independent of incentives and distortions of the prosecutor.
Similarly, the length of time between the lower court conviction and the appellate
decision can be taken into account. Define GAP as the number of months between the two
decisions. It seems reasonable to believe that frivolous appeals more likely occur soon after
the conviction and the decision is upheld.
Control variables for which type of court the conviction came from along with the
number ofjustices used in the appellate decision can be included. Define SUPREME as being
equal to 1 if the conviction came from the Supreme Court and let MISSING5 equal I if only
four justices are used. Furthermore, dummy variables controlling for which four/five
appellate court justices ruled on each case are created as well.

Along with this basic information, most slip opinions also provided information on
which crime was committed. Thirty-two dummy variables are created to control for the
various crimes committed. In situations where multiple crimes are committed, each is
coded. The controls do not differentiate between degrees or class. Over 95 percent of the
slip opinions provide information on the crime. The list of crimes and their descriptive
statistics are presented in the Appendix.
Additionally, the slip opinions provide information on the grounds of the appeal.
Numerous dummy variables are created. The method used to measure the grounds for the
appeal is as follows. For each slip opinion, only one ground for appeal (or 0) is coded.
Often, it is clear what the argument made by the appellant was. In these cases, only one
point was addressed by the justices and this was coded. Sometimes, though, multiple points
were discussed by the justices. Typically, the most relevant and important argument made
was addressed first and in more detail. When there is more than one point to be discussed,
the first one brought up by the justices in the appeal is the one used. A typical situation
would be where one dominant argument was addressed by the justices. In the last para-
graph, the justices will quickly dismiss the other points made. Other times, language such
as there being "no nonfrivolous points remaining" was used or that remaining points "lack
merit." Thirteen grounds dummy variables are created. Their list, along with the descriptive
statistics, is presented in the Appendix.
Also, there is variation in the type of defense employed by the defendant. Some
counties in western New York have created a public defender office. Low-income individ-
uals are eligible for this free legal defense. In the larger cities in the area (Buffalo,
Rochester, and Syracuse), legal aid organizations have been developed. Absent publicly
provided defense, a defendant may use a private attorney. It is worth pointing out, though,
that in rural counties that have not created a public defender office, the county is required
by state law to provide free legal assistance to low-income individuals. In these circum-
stances, a private attorney is also used. Six defense dummy variables are created to differ-
entiate the types of defense. Of these, 41.8 percent of the cases had private representation,
28.2 percent utilize public defenders, while the remaining defendants take advantage of
legal aid societies.

court's decision. The outcome provided in the slip opinion, though, is not binary. The

The final piece of information available in the slip opinion is the mode of the initial
trial. A conviction can be obtained with ajury trial or with a guilty plea. Additionally, New
York allows for nonjury (bench) trials that do not utilize a jury. Although a majority of
appeals come from guilty pleas, the fact that this percentage is as small as it is (55.6 percent)
is noteworthy. Typically, in state-level criminal courts a much higher proportion of convic-
tions arise from guilty pleas. For an example, in North Carolina over 97 percent of felony
convictions come this way (Bandyopadhyay & McCannon 2013). Thus, as to be expected,
appeals disproportionately come from convictions at trial. One likely reason for this is the
use of waiver of appeals within plea bargain agreements. Also, trials open up the possibility
of additional procedural errors to appeal.
Along with the information provided on the slip opinion, information on the pros-
ecutor, known in New York as the District Attorney, is collected. Information from each
county's Board of Elections is collected regarding both who is the current District Attorney
alongwith recent election results. District Attorneys run in partisan, popular elections in New
York and serve four-year terms. The information on elections is used to create the dummy
variable REELECT. Itis equal to 1 if the conviction occurs in the six months prior to the District
Attorney's reelection. Since the general election occurs in early November, REELECT is equal
to 1 in the months of May through October in the election year. It is important to emphasize
that REELECT is measuring the election concerns at the time of the initial conviction and not at
the time of the appeal. This is necessary to address the question as to whether, years later,
convictions that arose during reelection campaigns are more likely to be overturned by the
appellate courts. The mean value of this dummy variable in the data set is 0.098.
The counties in western New York stagger their elections. Figure 1 depicts the
number of District Attorney races that are in each election cycle. There is an uneven
staggering of elections in western New York.
Lower court judges in New York serve extended terms. County Court judges are
elected for 10-year terms, while Supreme Court justices serve 14-year terms. Thus, while
district attorney elections occur frequently in the data, convictions during times ofjudicial
retention concerns is rare. Furthermore, their election cycles do not coincide with pros-
ecutor elections.

more mistakes, which are corrected at the appellate level.

Modifications of sentences are more frequent than reversals of convictions. Appeals based
on a lack of a speedy trial are more successful when the conviction took place during a
reelection. Also, DWI, assault, and fraud convictions are more vulnerable to prosecutorial
zeal.
Appellate data provide the opportunity to evaluate formally the quality of the deci-
sions made in lower courts. Future work should exploit this further. The incentives faced by
judges, both at the court of first instance and at the appellate level, could be investigated.
This may be necessary so as to identify any potential interaction effect. The data used are
appeals of convictions. Although effort has been taken to deal with the resources available
to mount an appeal, obtaining data on all cases disposed, to identify which are appealed,
may provide further elaboration on the effect of prosecutor retention. Additionally, while
this study employs case-level data, even more detailed information on the specifics of the
case is needed to further elaborate on the way the outcomes of the courts are adjusted due
to election pressures. For example, is it the time devoted to campaigning that leads to errors
or is it zealousness at the bargaining table and at trial that directly drives the results? Other
than redesigning institutions, more detailed data would, presumably, allow for effective
monitoring. Similarly, while research has focused on the pressure on state-level prosecutors
caused by elections, a thorough policy analysis needs to also analyze appointment systems
so that the alternative can be contrasted. Finally, future work should consider the effect in
different states to ensure the results are not an anomaly of western New York.
