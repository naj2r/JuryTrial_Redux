PROSECUTORIAL RETENTION: SIGNALING BY TRIAL
SIDDHARTHA BANDYOPADHYAY
University of Birmingham
BRYAN C. McCANNON
Saint Bonaventure University
Abstract
We examine how retention motives affect prosecutor be-
havior under different evaluation criteria. In particular, we
analyze how prosecutors of differing capabilities respond
in choosing which cases to take to trial and which to plea
bargain. We show how different criteria distort the mix of
cases chosen for trial and that the direction of the distor-
tion depends crucially on the evaluation tool used. Optimal
evaluation metrics are derived that combine multiple sig-
nals of performance and are shown to achieve the ﬁrst-best
outcome.
1. Introduction

pointments, but instead face re-election.1 One may believe that having to
face such elections makes prosecutors responsive to the “will of the people.”

There is little formal work, though, on how the measures used to evaluate
prosecutors affect the outcomes of the criminal justice system.2 The few stud-
ies done suggest that cases brought to trial receive signiﬁcant weight in the
public’s judgment of a prosecutor’s quality (Wright 2009). We investigate
the question of whether evaluating prosecutors using commonly considered
indicators of quality such as success at trial and toughness of sentences ob-
tained lead to distortions in the types of cases brought for trial. Further, do
differing methods of evaluation lead to different types of distortions? Given
the preponderance of plea bargaining in the United States, we look, in par-
ticular, at how the mix of cases tried in court versus plea bargained responds
to how prosecutors are evaluated.
We ﬁnd that judging prosecutors on the basis of conviction rates in-
creases the number of cases that are plea bargained, while evaluating prose-
cutors on the length of the sentences obtained increases the number of cases
brought to trial. Further, we show that instead of deciding the re-election of
prosecutors on the basis of performance on one dimension if we evaluate
them along multiple dimensions (for example, giving them a target con-
viction rate and a target sentence length), one can discover the quality of
prosecutors and achieve ﬁrst-best outcomes.

aggregate sentence severity. In fact, among job performance metrics, these

idence surveyed in Wright [2009] for details).3 As ElectionLaw@Moritz notes,
“the political pressures on prosecutors has been said to lead to a subtle shift
away from the prosecutor’s goal of ‘doing justice’ and an increased focus
on conviction rates.”4 An extreme example of a prosecutor campaigning

The tradeoff prosecutors face is between a costly trial, which potentially
has a greater sanction (for a given level of evidence) but opens up the pos-
sibility of a failure, or plea bargain which saves the cost of trial and avoids
acquittal but leads to a lower sanction. Evaluating prosecutors on the basis
of their being able to obtain stiff sentences is shown to lead to an insufﬁcient
amount of plea bargaining and an excessive number of trials. Desiring to
be retained, prosecutors increase their aggregate sentence length by taking
cases to trial where the difference between the expected sanction from trial
and that obtained via plea bargaining is too small to justify the costs. Alterna-
tively, if success in court is used to measure the prosecutor’s quality, then an
excessive amount of plea bargaining arises and too few cases are brought to
trial. A prosecutor, to improve her conviction rate, may withhold cases from
trial where the gain in the expected sanction from court is otherwise justiﬁed
given the costs.
This leads one to ask whether there exists a “best metric” to evaluate
prosecutors, and if so what form it takes. In particular, can the voting
public design an optimal mechanism to achieve ﬁrst-best (socially optimal)
outcomes simply by looking at data on trials? It is shown that rather than
focusing on a single criterion, such as probability of success or toughness
of sentences, using multiple measures to evaluate prosecutors can lead to
separation by quality type and, more importantly, can achieve the ﬁrst-best
outcome. Speciﬁcally, if used in combination with each other the two criteria
considered can eliminate the distortions and achieve the optimal outcome.
For example, the conviction rate a higher quality prosecutor obtains in the
ﬁrst-best outcome can be required by the voter. A lower skilled prosecutor
would have to withhold many cases from court to achieve this rate of success.
As a consequence, the aggregate sentence length achieved would be reduced
since many cases would be plea bargained at lower sentences. Therefore,
also requiring a sufﬁciently high aggregate sentence length successfully sep-
arates high and low quality leading them to choose the optimal amount of
prosecution.
This result emphasizes that the focus on a single signal of quality such
as success in trial, rather than overall performance (where we use multiple
measures or signals), leads to a bias in the optimal mix of cases tried and plea
bargained. Thus, we agree with Wright (2009) that voters focusing on only

The distortion induced by focusing on conviction rates to evaluate pros-
ecutors is discussed in Rasmusen, Raghav, and Ramseyer (2009). They argue
that any system that pays attention to conviction rate rather than number
of convictions is liable to be abused. However, using the number of cases
as a criterion as they suggest will lead to strategically choosing to prose-
cute on the lowest and most easily provable charges. Bibas (2009) acknowl-
edges that evaluation tools are incomplete and manipulable. He proposes
performance-based compensation to encourage line prosecutors to serve
their constituents. Boylan (2005) analyzes data of chief federal prosecutors
and their subsequent careers. He presents results that suggest prosecutors
maximize sentence length as opposed to conviction rates. Glaeser, Kessler,
and Morrison Piehl (2000) ﬁnd evidence that career concerns affect the de-
cisions of U.S. attorneys and, speciﬁcally, has led to an increase in the feder-
alization of drug crimes. Thus, along with distortions caused by incomplete
tools and career concerns, it is argued here that retention motivations affect
the prosecutors’ decisions. In an environment where a prosecutor selects the
amount of effort to exert, Garoupa (2009) argues that mandatory prosecu-
tion might well be more appropriate than selective prosecution. Another pa-
per which has close connections with our work is Gordon and Huber (2002)
who consider a moral hazard problem where voters use observable signals
of performance to induce prosecutorial effort. Unlike our model, adverse
section issues are overlooked in their framework. Our analysis ignores moral
hazard and hence our analysis in many ways complements their work. Both
are important issues and studying each in isolation allows us to pinpoint the
tradeoffs better.
This is not the ﬁrst work to consider signaling in the pretrial activities
of agents in the judicial system. Reinganum (1988) models the prosecutor’s
choice between dismissal, plea bargaining, and prosecution at trial by con-
sidering the asymmetric information regarding the strength of the evidence
known by the prosecutor. The plea offer made by the prosecutor itself acts
as a signal. Reinganum (2000) considers the opportunity for a defendant to
use his offer during plea bargaining to signal the severity of the harm he
caused, which may be revealed during the trial. These models are used to
evaluate the effect of policy (restrictions to prosecutorial discretion and sen-
tencing guidelines respectively) on the plea bargaining process. Here we are
interested on how retention policies affect such choices.
The results also contribute to the extensive literature on plea bargain-
ing. Plea bargaining’s value has been argued to reduce the costs of the legal
system (Landes 1971), screen guilty from innocent defendants (Baker and

incumbent challenger models (see Besley [2006] for an overview of the lit-

both papers, worker qualities do not differ; thus they are not concerned with

product liability and employment discrimination lawsuits. Judges may also
be inﬂuenced by retention motivations. Shepherd (2009) considers the vot-

inﬂuenced by the preferences of the retention agents, whether they be the
political afﬁliation of the governor of the state for appointed judges or the
preferences of the median voter for elected ones. Hanssen (1999) argues
that knowledge of how court decisions will affect powerful groups, who pro-

2. Model
Consider a two-period model. In the ﬁrst period there is a single prosecutor
of unknown quality who is to decide how to handle cases brought before
her. To keep the analysis simple suppose the prosecutor is one of two quality
types q ∈{H, L} which we call high and low, respectively. She is high quality
with probability γ ∈(0, 1) and low quality with probability 1 −γ . Let the
parameter θ denote the strength of evidence she has against the defendant
in any given case. One may also think of θ being dependent on the skill
of the defendant’s lawyer and the investigatorial expenditures made by the

2.2. The Model with Asymmetric Information
With perfect information the ﬁrst best occurs; high-quality prosecutors, who
are more likely to be successful in the courtroom, should be retained, but
low-quality prosecutors should be replaced. With asymmetric information,
voters no longer know the type of prosecutor in ofﬁce though they know the
probability that the prosecutor is high type. Given that there is a bonus for
being retained, even a civic-minded prosecutor’s payoff no longer coincides
with the voter and thus with asymmetric information about quality, all types
of prosecutors may try to signal they are of a high type in order to ensure
re-election. We are interested in understanding how such signals distort the
outcomes in the criminal justice system and if there is a remedy.
Before the incentives of the prosecutors are addressed, recall that for
cases where θ > θL welfare maximization requires that both high-quality
and low-quality prosecutors go to trial, while for cases where θ ≤θH both
types should plea bargain. Only if θL ≥θ > θH does optimal behavior
differ—high-quality prosecutors should take the case to trial while low-
quality prosecutors should plea bargain. Since the former is more successful
in court only her surplus from trial (the difference between the expected
sentence and the sanction that can be achieved through plea bargaining)
justiﬁes the court costs. Suppose the quality of the prosecutor is unobserv-
able to the voting population. In that case, we would like to investigate if

be the best that can be achieved under asymmetric information. We now
consider what happens if the median voter uses a combination of the two
criteria to evaluate the prosecutor. Is there a metric that combines the two

probability of success out of the courtroom. Hence, too few cases go to trial.

dimension, by requiring the prosecutor to succeed in both dimensions, a
separating equilibrium in which prosecutors of all levels of skill select the
ﬁrst-best outcome can be achieved.

Under asymmetric information, the equilibria that arise result in a semi-

as the metric to evaluate prosecutorial quality. Suppose the median voter re-

disregard retention and select the ﬁrst-best amount of prosecution. For those
with values of q close to q ′ it is best to distort up their decision to go to trial
to achieve X ′. For those with sufﬁciently low values of q such a choice is not
optimal. As a result, the equilibrium can be described as separation among
those prosecutors with very low values of q, pooling among those with inter-
mediate values of q, and separation (and retention) of those with very high
values of q.

6. Conclusion
A number of measures can be used to evaluate the performance of pub-
lic prosecutors. Such tools serve as signals to the retention agents convey-
ing information on the unknown skill of the incumbent. This opens up the
possibility of distortions in the criminal justice system. In a simple theo-
retical model the nature of these potential distortions is investigated. Two
evaluation criteria are considered and each, independently, is shown to
affect the number of cases taken to court relative to those plea bargained

with the direction of the effect depending on which is used. Thus, depend-
ing on the metric used signaling could lead to distortions in either direc-
tion, i.e., we could have too many or too few criminal trials. Importantly, it
is shown that a ﬁrst-best outcome is possible. To achieve this one combines
information that the conviction rate and the aggregate sentence length con-
vey to effectively distinguish high-quality and low-quality prosecutors. It is
worth mentioning that the reason why a combination of two metrics work is
because they cause distortions in opposite directions.
Some robustness issues are worth mentioning. The assumption in our
model that there are trials under the ﬁrst best is not crucial for our signal-
ing story. In Bandyopadhyay and McCannon (2013) we show that, even when
plea bargaining is optimal for both prosecutor and defendant, trials are used
to signal quality. It has also been suggested that cases with very weak evi-
dence as well as those with very strong evidence are taken to trial with cases
in the middle being plea bargained (see Priest and Klein 1984). In Appendix
A, we show that even with such double crossing, our results go through. In
Appendix B, we show that even when the plea outcome depends on prose-
cutor type, our results remain essentially unchanged. Appendix C shows our
results survive even when Type I and Type II errors are introduced.
One can argue that there are some important issues we do not consider.
First, as trials use up more resources than plea bargaining, the number of
plea bargains may indeed affect the outcomes in trials—if there are fewer
trials the prosecutor may be able to invest more in them so the outcomes in
the courtroom itself depends on the number of cases plea bargained. While
this adds complexity in our analysis, the effects of the signaling mechanism
should go through as high-quality prosecutors will still be able to achieve
better outcomes for the same number of trials. Thus, trials again become
a signal of quality which may lead to distortions. The environment consid-
ered also does not incorporate ideology. In that case the tradeoffs may differ
across prosecutors of similar quality but different ideology, who vary in how
they value sanctions. For example, one would still expect to see distortions
(from the median voter) induced by elections once one controls for ideol-
ogy. Additionally, the assistant prosecutors who are hired by the elected chief
prosecutor may have objectives that differ substantially. They may be inter-
ested in improving themselves in the external labor market. For example,
a less experienced attorney may be interested in taking a particular case to
trial to gain courtroom experience that is valuable to private sector law ﬁrms.
Also, there are numerous choices made by prosecutors not considered in
the analysis. Examples include the decision of whether to ﬁle charges, which
charges to ﬁle, the allocation of cases within a prosecutor’s ofﬁce, the in-
clusion of lesser offenses, and whether convictions as charged are achieved.
Expanding the environment will enrich the analysis, but should preserve the
distortions created by the evaluation process, which is the primary result
identiﬁed here. Similarly, another important feature of prosecutorial per-
formance is the effort exerted. Bibas (2009) emphasizes the importance of

The analysis does not explicitly consider the deterrent impact of punish-

of the legislature in determining optimal sanctions and the ex post objec-

paper, which analyzes departures from the ﬁrst best arising from re-election

The work highlights the potential distortions caused by reasonable evalu-
ation tools and describes the optimal combination that can achieve ﬁrst best.
The existence of an optimal mix of evaluation tools does not mean that it has
been used to elect prosecutors in practice. Potentially it falls on the candi-
dates themselves, the media, and special interest groups to bring to light the
merits and deﬁciencies of the incumbent prosecutor by highlighting their
performance in multiple dimensions. In particular, the voting public proba-
bly pays maximum attention to cases when election time comes near which
is why we expect the distortion to be mainly during the election year. Also,
prosecutors early on in their career may be more subject to scrutiny than
later on in their career. Clearly, detailed empirical evidence of prosecutorial
behavior with respect to election cycles is needed to see if they create dis-
tortions or whether an optimal use of multiple criteria has led to optimal
selection of prosecutors. These issues are left for future investigation.
