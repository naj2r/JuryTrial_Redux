3.1.2. Pooling Equilibrium
Of course, this is not the only type of equilibrium possible. It may well be
that pooling equilibria exist. In a pooling equilibria both types achieve an
aggregate sentence length X e and the retention ofﬁcial believes that a pros-
ecutor who achieves X e or above is type H with probability γ and type L with
probability 1 −γ . What outcomes can arise in such an equilibrium?
First, a high-quality prosecutor if retained ﬁnds the ﬁrst-best outcome,
XH, most desirable since her preferences include society’s well-being. If the
retention agents attempt to achieve a pooling outcome at an aggregate sen-
tence length less than this level, then a high-quality prosecutor simply de-
viates to the ﬁrst-best outcome and retains her position. Thus, there is no
pooling equilibrium where X e < XH.
Hence, consider outcomes where the voters require the aggregate
sentence length be greater than or equal to XH to be retained. For such

REMARK 2:
Contrary to our result on separating equilibria, all pooling equilibria
in the range [XH, X φ
L ] satisfy the Intuitive Criteria. This contrasts with the result in
Spence’s model. The reason why this survives the intuitive criteria is that in the pooling
equilibrium, both types are retained and the high type is already choosing an aggregate
sentence length that is to the right of her ﬁrst best. Thus, the high type has no incentive
to deviate as she is already getting the highest possible reward. In the Spence model the
high type by deviating can get a higher wage if the employer believes she is of high type
which gives her an incentive to deviate.

opposite. Second-period welfare is higher under separation. Thus, we can-
not say which is better for welfare, though it is possible to calculate the best
equilibrium for the voters for different parameters of the model.
