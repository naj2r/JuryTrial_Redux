and negotiate a plea a prosecutor may choose not to and take the case to

greater sanction arising in court improves her record and aids in her re-
election. While many have emphasized the incentives placed on the defen-
dant’s lawyer (Delacote and Ancelot 2009, Leshem 2009, Ferrer 2011), the
results highlight the need to consider the prosecutor’s incentives as well.
In terms of modeling, this paper has similarities with the literature on
incumbent challenger models (see Besley [2006] for an overview of the lit-
erature) which looks at the agency issues involved in the election of elected
representatives. Such models have been used to analyze a host of situations
including political business cycles (Rogoff and Siebert 1988, Rogoff 1990),
persistence of inefﬁcient policy (Majumdar and Mukand 2004), transfers to
interest groups (Coate and Morris 1995), and persistence of conﬂict (Bandy-
opadhyay and Oak 2010). Similar to our model, this literature examines what
distortions arise as a result of trying to understand the quality of politicians
from the actions they take in ofﬁce. Our model predicts an excessive be-
havior in line with most results in the literature. However, unlike most such
models we are able to use a combination of metrics (essentially using the
fact that the signals they generate are correlated), which give us the ﬁrst-
best outcome. In that sense, there is some similarity with the multi-tasking
model analyzed by H¨olmstrom and Milgrom (1991). A related issue is con-
sidered in Baker, Gibbons, and Murphy (1994) who show how combining
subjective performance measures with objective evaluations improves prof-
its over that achieved by simply using objective measures. Their distortion
comes from contractual incompleteness and not asymmetric information. In
both papers, worker qualities do not differ; thus they are not concerned with
selecting the best types of workers.
Finally, the work relates to the investigation of judicial distortions. Ide-
ology of the judges has been shown to affect trial outcomes. For example,
Taha (2010) provides evidence that the mix of Democratic and Republican
appointed federal judges in a district affects the choice to ﬁle cases such as
product liability and employment discrimination lawsuits. Judges may also
be inﬂuenced by retention motivations. Shepherd (2009) considers the vot-
ing of state supreme court judges and shows that the behavior of judges is
inﬂuenced by the preferences of the retention agents, whether they be the
political afﬁliation of the governor of the state for appointed judges or the
preferences of the median voter for elected ones. Hanssen (1999) argues
that knowledge of how court decisions will affect powerful groups, who pro-
vide support for elected judges, narrows the range of likely rulings. This di-
minishes the uncertainty and is shown to decrease the amount of litigation.
