PROSECUTORIAL RETENTION: SIGNALING BY TRIAL
SIDDHARTHA BANDYOPADHYAY
University of Birmingham
BRYAN C. McCANNON
Saint Bonaventure University
Abstract
We examine how retention motives affect prosecutor be-
havior under different evaluation criteria. In particular, we
analyze how prosecutors of differing capabilities respond
in choosing which cases to take to trial and which to plea
bargain. We show how different criteria distort the mix of
cases chosen for trial and that the direction of the distor-
tion depends crucially on the evaluation tool used. Optimal
evaluation metrics are derived that combine multiple sig-
nals of performance and are shown to achieve the ﬁrst-best
outcome.

There is little formal work, though, on how the measures used to evaluate
prosecutors affect the outcomes of the criminal justice system.2 The few stud-
ies done suggest that cases brought to trial receive signiﬁcant weight in the
public’s judgment of a prosecutor’s quality (Wright 2009). We investigate
the question of whether evaluating prosecutors using commonly considered
indicators of quality such as success at trial and toughness of sentences ob-
tained lead to distortions in the types of cases brought for trial. Further, do
differing methods of evaluation lead to different types of distortions? Given
the preponderance of plea bargaining in the United States, we look, in par-
ticular, at how the mix of cases tried in court versus plea bargained responds
to how prosecutors are evaluated.
We ﬁnd that judging prosecutors on the basis of conviction rates in-
creases the number of cases that are plea bargained, while evaluating prose-
cutors on the length of the sentences obtained increases the number of cases
brought to trial. Further, we show that instead of deciding the re-election of
prosecutors on the basis of performance on one dimension if we evaluate
them along multiple dimensions (for example, giving them a target con-
viction rate and a target sentence length), one can discover the quality of
prosecutors and achieve ﬁrst-best outcomes.

considered measures of success at trial affect the decision of a prosecutor

the metric used, as we show below.
The tradeoff prosecutors face is between a costly trial, which potentially
has a greater sanction (for a given level of evidence) but opens up the pos-
sibility of a failure, or plea bargain which saves the cost of trial and avoids
acquittal but leads to a lower sanction. Evaluating prosecutors on the basis
of their being able to obtain stiff sentences is shown to lead to an insufﬁcient
amount of plea bargaining and an excessive number of trials. Desiring to
be retained, prosecutors increase their aggregate sentence length by taking
cases to trial where the difference between the expected sanction from trial
and that obtained via plea bargaining is too small to justify the costs. Alterna-
tively, if success in court is used to measure the prosecutor’s quality, then an
excessive amount of plea bargaining arises and too few cases are brought to
trial. A prosecutor, to improve her conviction rate, may withhold cases from
trial where the gain in the expected sanction from court is otherwise justiﬁed
given the costs.
This leads one to ask whether there exists a “best metric” to evaluate
prosecutors, and if so what form it takes. In particular, can the voting
public design an optimal mechanism to achieve ﬁrst-best (socially optimal)
outcomes simply by looking at data on trials? It is shown that rather than
focusing on a single criterion, such as probability of success or toughness
of sentences, using multiple measures to evaluate prosecutors can lead to
separation by quality type and, more importantly, can achieve the ﬁrst-best
outcome. Speciﬁcally, if used in combination with each other the two criteria
considered can eliminate the distortions and achieve the optimal outcome.
For example, the conviction rate a higher quality prosecutor obtains in the
ﬁrst-best outcome can be required by the voter. A lower skilled prosecutor
would have to withhold many cases from court to achieve this rate of success.
As a consequence, the aggregate sentence length achieved would be reduced
since many cases would be plea bargained at lower sentences. Therefore,
also requiring a sufﬁciently high aggregate sentence length successfully sep-
arates high and low quality leading them to choose the optimal amount of
prosecution.
This result emphasizes that the focus on a single signal of quality such
as success in trial, rather than overall performance (where we use multiple
measures or signals), leads to a bias in the optimal mix of cases tried and plea
bargained. Thus, we agree with Wright (2009) that voters focusing on only

The distortion induced by focusing on conviction rates to evaluate pros-
ecutors is discussed in Rasmusen, Raghav, and Ramseyer (2009). They argue
that any system that pays attention to conviction rate rather than number
of convictions is liable to be abused. However, using the number of cases
as a criterion as they suggest will lead to strategically choosing to prose-
cute on the lowest and most easily provable charges. Bibas (2009) acknowl-
edges that evaluation tools are incomplete and manipulable. He proposes
performance-based compensation to encourage line prosecutors to serve
their constituents. Boylan (2005) analyzes data of chief federal prosecutors
and their subsequent careers. He presents results that suggest prosecutors
maximize sentence length as opposed to conviction rates. Glaeser, Kessler,
and Morrison Piehl (2000) ﬁnd evidence that career concerns affect the de-
cisions of U.S. attorneys and, speciﬁcally, has led to an increase in the feder-
alization of drug crimes. Thus, along with distortions caused by incomplete
tools and career concerns, it is argued here that retention motivations affect
the prosecutors’ decisions. In an environment where a prosecutor selects the
amount of effort to exert, Garoupa (2009) argues that mandatory prosecu-
tion might well be more appropriate than selective prosecution. Another pa-
per which has close connections with our work is Gordon and Huber (2002)
who consider a moral hazard problem where voters use observable signals
of performance to induce prosecutorial effort. Unlike our model, adverse
section issues are overlooked in their framework. Our analysis ignores moral
hazard and hence our analysis in many ways complements their work. Both
are important issues and studying each in isolation allows us to pinpoint the
tradeoffs better.
This is not the ﬁrst work to consider signaling in the pretrial activities
of agents in the judicial system. Reinganum (1988) models the prosecutor’s
choice between dismissal, plea bargaining, and prosecution at trial by con-
sidering the asymmetric information regarding the strength of the evidence
known by the prosecutor. The plea offer made by the prosecutor itself acts
as a signal. Reinganum (2000) considers the opportunity for a defendant to
use his offer during plea bargaining to signal the severity of the harm he
caused, which may be revealed during the trial. These models are used to
evaluate the effect of policy (restrictions to prosecutorial discretion and sen-
tencing guidelines respectively) on the plea bargaining process. Here we are
interested on how retention policies affect such choices.
