the following components:

where the magnitude of each depends on the size of the sanction im-
posed. Assume that each of these functions is continuously differentiable. An
obvious assumption is that correct decisions outweigh mistakes: a(S) > b(S)
and c(S) > d(S). Additionally, as is common in criminal justice arguments,
assume that mistaken convictions are more costly to society than mistaken
acquittals: a(S) −b(S) > c(S) −d(S). Of course, b(S) and d(S) can be < 0
as is usually assumed but that is not necessary for our analysis.
Furthermore, deﬁne εq as the probability an error is made at trial by an
agent of quality q. To simplify the analysis assume that the error rate does
not depend on the underlying state (i.e., guilt or innocence).
Additionally, convictions at trial may result in positive spillovers. For
one example, each conviction adds to the deterrence effect of the threat-
ened sanction. Also, greater sanctions from trial result in more costs due to
incarceration. Finally, it is possible that a beneﬁt to each trial is the fur-
ther development of criminal law and procedure. Consequently, deﬁne Z as
the aggregate, net beneﬁt of these effects, that is, the amount of additional
social welfare generated from trial convictions beyond that generated by plea
bargains.
Going to court results in either a conviction or an acquittal. The
expected welfare generated from each is
Eω (conviction) = (1 −εq)a (S) + εqb (S) + Z
Eω (acquittal) = (1 −εq)c (S) + εqd (S).
Consequently, the expected welfare generated by going to trial is
Eω (trial) = p q (θ) [(1 −εq)a (S) + εqb (S) + Z]
+(1 −p q (θ))[(1 −εq)c (S) + εqd (S)].
This contrasts with the text where Ew(trial) = p q(θ)w(S).

Using the minimal assumptions made regarding the welfare functions
a number of reasonable results arise regarding the decision to go to trial.
First, the expected welfare from going to trial decreases as mistakes become
more prevalent. Second, the positive spillovers to trial convictions increase
the expected welfare of trials making them more desirable. Third, the impact
of the effect of improved evidence and increased likelihood of conviction on
the expected welfare from going to trial depends on the magnitude of the
errors. So long as the value to gaining a correct conviction is not too small,
a(S) > c(S), there exists a cutoff value of εq, εq, for which if the likelihood of
a mistake is less than the threshold then the value of going to trial improves
when the probability of conviction increases, but if the mistake probability
exceeds the threshold then the expected welfare from going to trial worsens
when this probability increases. In other words, the natural result arises that
convictions are a good thing when mistakes are not too great.
