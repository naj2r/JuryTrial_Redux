REMARK 3:
As in the case with aggregate sentence all pooling equilibria survive
the Intuitive Criteria while only the best separating equilibrium does.
While it may be natural to think of prosecutors signaling quality using
either of these criteria, it is possible to think of the voters setting criteria for
prosecutors as well. With a single criteria, the same distortions occur except
that the screening equilibrium will generate the equilibrium which generates
the highest payoff for the voter. Thus, the payoffs from the best separating
and the best pooling equilibrium will be compared and the one with the
higher payoffs will be the outcome in the equivalent screening game.
4. Optimal Evaluation Criteria and First Best
The previous analysis identiﬁes the outcomes that can be achieved if only
a single metric is used to evaluate the prosecutor. However, this may not
be the best that can be achieved under asymmetric information. We now
consider what happens if the median voter uses a combination of the two
criteria to evaluate the prosecutor. Is there a metric that combines the two
measurements to improve total welfare?

Thus, we look at the resultant screening problem for the voter and
show that there indeed exists an optimal evaluation metric. In fact, in this
environment there exists a set of cutoff measures that can achieve the ﬁrst-
best outcome. The intuition for this result is as follows. As shown in the
previous analysis, an important feature of the two evaluation tools (aggre-
gate sentence length and conviction rate) is that they, independently, lead
to distortions in opposing directions. The use of aggregate sentence length
encourages prosecutors to inﬂate this measurement by taking to court those
cases where the expected sanction is greater than could be achieved via plea
bargaining, but may not be justiﬁed given the additional costs incurred. In
short, too many cases go to trial. The use of the conviction record provides
prosecutors the incentive to inﬂate this metric by keeping cases with a lower
probability of success out of the courtroom. Hence, too few cases go to trial.
Since success in a dimension comes at the expense of success in the other
dimension, by requiring the prosecutor to succeed in both dimensions, a
separating equilibrium in which prosecutors of all levels of skill select the
ﬁrst-best outcome can be achieved.
