## SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION & CONTEXT

### Central Claim
Hessick & Morse challenge the conventional wisdom that prosecutor elections are "empty exercises." They show:
- Incumbents almost always win when they run (95% win rate confirmed)
- **BUT:** The real issue is lack of contestation, not incumbent strength
- **Critical finding:** Results vary dramatically by **population size** — not a monolithic phenomenon

### The Progressive Prosecutor Movement Context
- Rise of electoral reform movement (Soros funding, Real Justice PAC, ACLU Vote Smart Justice)
- Goal: Use local elections to elect "progressive prosecutors" committed to less punitive criminal justice
- **Key assumption under scrutiny:** Can elections actually change prosecutorial behavior?
- **Problem identified:** Reform efforts concentrate on urban/suburban districts, neglecting rural areas where contestation is almost impossible

## SECTION 2: DATA & METHODOLOGY

### Dataset Scope
- **2,315 prosecutor districts** across 45 states
- Election cycles from 2012–2017 (mostly 2014–2016)
- Comprehensive data collection: candidate names, party, incumbency, vote totals, district population, urbanicity, prison admission rates
- **Data completeness:**

  - 70% have year incumbent first assumed office

### Key Definitions
| Term | Definition | Importance |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Contested** | 2+ candidates in an election | Minimal bar — doesn't mean real choice |
| **Competitive** | Winner < 60% of vote | Better measure of actual competition |
| **Open seat** | Incumbent did not seek reelection | Different dynamics from incumbent races |
| **"Either election"** | Contested in primary OR general | Accounts for one-party-dominant jurisdictions |

## SECTION 3: CORE FINDING — POPULATION AS PRIMARY PREDICTOR

### Table 5 Summary: Contestation Rates by Population

**General Election Contestation:**
- **Smallest districts (< 100,000 pop):** 13% contested
- **Medium districts (100k–250k):** ~18% contested
- **Large districts (250k–1M):** 21% contested
- **Largest districts (1M+):** 34% contested

**Overall pattern:** ~2.6× higher contestation in largest vs. smallest districts

### Open Seat Elections (Critical for Supply Hypothesis)
- **All jurisdictions with 1M+ population:** 100% contestation rate (open seats ALWAYS contested)
- **250k–1M population:** Open seats contested ~70% of time
- **100k–250k population:** Open seats contested ~70% of time
- **Smallest districts (< 100k):** Open seats contested **only 50%** of the time

**Implication:** Even when no incumbent runs, small-population districts struggle to field candidates. This is NOT just an incumbency advantage problem.

### Competition (Actual Closeness)
Among contested elections:
- Smallest districts: 59% are competitive (< 60% winner margin)
- Largest districts: 75% are competitive
- **Conclusion:** Larger districts have both more contested AND more competitive elections

## SECTION 4: THE "SUPPLY PROBLEM HYPOTHESIS" — PRIMARY MECHANISM

### Core Insight
Contestation is driven **primarily by supply of eligible candidates**, not solely by incumbency advantage or voter apathy.

### Evidence for Supply Constraint

**Extreme Cases:**
- 16 jurisdictions nationally could not field **even a single candidate** for prosecutor
- Forced to **appoint** prosecutor instead of holding election
- All 16 counties had population **< 7,000** (most < 1,000)
- 4 additional counties: no candidates registered, election decided by write-in

**Examples:**
- Custer County, Idaho: Only qualified candidate failed bar exam → no attorneys lived in county
- Hamlin County, South Dakota: Only 1 attorney in county, he works for bank, uninterested
- This is not hypothetical dysfunction — it's systemic in very rural areas

### Table 8 & Table 9: Lawyer Supply as Mechanism

**Distribution of lawyers per district (10-state sample):**
| Metric | Small districts (< 100k pop) | All districts |
|--------|-----|-----|
| 25th percentile | 4 lawyers | 6 lawyers |
| 50th percentile (median) | 16 lawyers | 31 lawyers |
| 75th percentile | ~50+ lawyers | — |

**Contestation correlation with lawyer supply (Table 9 — small districts only):**
- **< 5 lawyers:** 9.6% chance of contested election
- **5–15 lawyers:** 18.5% chance
- **16–45 lawyers:** 25.9% chance
- **46+ lawyers:** 30% chance

**Interpretation:** Even among small districts, lawyer supply matters. Doubling lawyers from 5 to 15+ nearly **doubles** contestation likelihood.

### Geographic Variation in Lawyer Supply
Non-linear relationship with population:
- Some large counties have fewer lawyers than smaller counties (e.g., Mariposa vs. Mono, California)
- Lawyers cluster geographically — economic concentrations matter
- State-level variation: New York (1 lawyer per 153 people) vs. Nebraska (1 per 339 people)

## SECTION 5: PROPOSED SOLUTIONS & TRADEOFFS

### Option 1: Residency Exception Reforms
**Approach:** Allow out-of-district lawyers to run (weaken residency restrictions)

**Evidence:**
- Nebraska permits lawyers in adjoining counties to run in counties with no attorney residents
- Result: Moderately successful at filling seats, but **does not increase contested elections**

**Assessment:** One-way fix (prevents dead seats), but doesn't create competition

---

### Option 2: District Consolidation (Multi-County Districts)
**Approach:** Merge small counties into larger districts to increase lawyer pool

**Case Studies:**

**Kentucky (120 counties → 57 districts):**
- 20 counties have < 10 lawyers, 36 have 10–19 lawyers
- After consolidation: Only 1 district has < 20 lawyers
- Contestation rate: **19%** (baseline)

**South Carolina (46 counties → 16 districts):**
- 1 prosecutor per 289,000 people
- All districts have 120+ lawyers
- Contestation rate: **25%** (6 percentage points higher than Kentucky)

**Comparison:**
- South Carolina's larger consolidation creates higher contestation
- Effect is real but **smaller than expected** given lawyer supply differences
- Suggests supply is necessary but not sufficient condition

---

### Option 3: County as "Artificial Construct" Argument
**Historical perspective:**
- Prosecutor elections are not ancestrally tied to county boundaries
- 19th-century single-county rule was adopted because long-distance travel was impractical (horse/carriage)
- Modern transportation undermines original rationale
- County boundaries drawn before settlement patterns emerged — don't delineate real communities

**Political implication:** Less resistance to consolidation than might be expected if counties were truly meaningful units

---

### Tradeoff: Local Control vs. Resources
Larger districts provide:
- ✓ More lawyer candidates
- ✓ Higher contestation rates
- ✗ Less local participation and voice

Smaller districts provide:
- ✓ Greater community voice
- ✓ More responsive to local concerns
- ✗ Fewer qualified candidates
- ✗ Uncontested elections (no choice)

**Hessick & Morse view:** Uncontested elections (no choice) are worse than lack of "voice" in larger districts.

## SECTION 6: THE ELECTION EFFECT & BEHAVIORAL RESPONSE

### Political Business Cycle Literature

**Theory:** Prosecutors behave more punitively in election years to maximize conviction rates (assumed voter preference)

**Status:** Literature is mixed and small:
- Some studies find prosecutors dismiss fewer cases before elections (more convictions)
- Other studies find increased dismissals (less convictions)
- One study finds "weak" reduction in dismissals
- No consensus yet

### Hessick & Morse's Finding
**Counterintuitive result:**
- Prosecutors **least likely to face contested elections** (small/rural jurisdictions) **act more punitively as a general matter**
- This contradicts election-effect theory
- Suggests: Lack of electoral pressure → more punitive behavior, OR election pressure → more measured behavior

**Implication for your jury study:** If Δ ≈ 0 for jury outcomes, it could be because:
1. Prosecutors do respond to electoral pressure, but jury outcomes are not the margin they adjust
2. Prosecution behavior response is on other margins (plea bargaining, charge selection, trial strategy, not jury composition/contestation)
3. Jury demand is elastic to caseload/trial pressure, not prosecutorial electoral incentives

## SECTION 7: PROGRESSIVE PROSECUTOR MOVEMENT CAVEATS

### How Progressives Win
**Conditions for progressive challenger success:**
1. **External funding** (Soros backing, organized PACs)
   - Larry Krasner: ~$1.5M spent
   - Eric Gonzalez: Raised 6× more than nearest competitor
   - Scott Colom: Raised 2× incumbent's contributions

2. **Changed campaign rhetoric**
   - Shifted from "qualifications + conviction rates" to **policy platform**
   - Emphasize racial disparities, diversion programs, prosecutorial discretion
   - Attacks on prosecutorial misconduct (Ken Thompson's 2013 Brooklyn victory)

3. **Unusual circumstances**
   - Philadelphia: 7-candidate open-seat primary (unprecedented crowding)
   - Brooklyn: Incumbent resigned amid corruption charges

### Why Progressives Can Lose
**Same tactics are available to law-and-order prosecutors:**
- Sacramento 2018: Law enforcement groups funded Anne Marie Schubert's incumbent defense
- San Diego 2018: Summer Stephan painted progressive challenger as "anti-prosecutor," "endangering public safety"
- Increased contestation ≠ progressive victory inevitable

**Cautionary analogy:** Judicial elections became more contested in late 20th century, but business interests' increased spending shifted outcomes toward pro-business judges (Kang & Shepherd). Similar dynamic possible in prosecutor elections if law-and-order groups mobilize.

### Implications for Your Jury Verdict Δ ≈ 0 Effect

**Hessick & Morse's framework suggests:**

1. **Structural hypothesis:** Michigan's ~18% contestation is driven by population/lawyer supply, NOT prosecutorial performance or behavior
   - This means contestation is **relatively exogenous** to what the prosecutor is doing
   - Supports your interpretation that behavioral response (jury strategy) might not be the operative channel

2. **Electoral calendar hypothesis:** If contestation is determined structurally, prosecutors' electoral incentives activate based on **cycle timing**, not **performance-based threats**
   - Your T1 incumbent-running effect (Δ ≈ 0) could reflect: prosecutors know they face X% chance of being challenged regardless of jury outcomes
   - Jury verdicts might not be the margin where this plays out (plea bargaining, charge selection, trial frequency might matter more)

3. **Rural vs. urban divergence:** Hessick & Morse show rural prosecutors (< 13% contestation, high incarceration) are most insulated from electoral pressure
   - If you see opposite effect in rural vs. urban Michigan counties, it would support **supply-driven contestation** story
   - Example: Rural prosecutor running unopposed has no electoral pressure re: jury strategy; urban prosecutor faces threat even if performance is identical
